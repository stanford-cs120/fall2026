What makes an evaluation valid?

Class meeting: 2026-11-10

Module 5: What makes an evaluation valid?

This module is about the role of institutions in assuring AI systems. Part of that role is to provide epistemic assurance: producing valid evaluations of AI systems, often in terms of their capabilities and hazards. Another role is to establish and enforce safeguards intended to keep the residual risk of developing and deploying AI systems at acceptable levels.

The role of the institution extends beyond evaluation validity to institutional accountability, then to information hazards, disclosure, and access to evidence. An institution's assurance claims may inform the decisions of their own, or another institution. This mode of operation can create systematic consequences for failures of measurement, governance, and transparency.

Epistemic failures need not be intentional or reflective of bad actors. Many sociotechnical systems involve unobservable theoretical constructs. So far in this course we've discussed examples including preferences, toxicity, and sycophancy. Measuring them has required modeling and inference from observable properties thought to be related to the underlying construct. Construct-operationalization enables this inference, but necessarily relies on assumptions. The fact that a sociotechnical system concerns an unobservable theoretical construct does not imply that the system must estimate that construct. This note asks when such inference is necessary, warranted, or useful compared to observable measurement.

Construct Observable evidence Modeling assumption
Preference Choices or rankings Utility axioms apply to revealed choices
Toxicity Annotator labels Labels represent intended notion of toxicity
Sycophancy Response given A/B framing Contrast identifies excessive compliance

Measurement modeling, see examples above, make these assumptions explicit and some implications testable. Indeed, measurement modeling as a framework can help support explanations, anticipation, and mitigation of some sociotechnical harms. This can be cast as an argument with two premises and a proposed conclusion:

P1: Many sociotechnical harms result from construct-operationalization mismatches

P2: Measurement modeling makes these assumptions explicit and some implications testable

C1: Measurement modeling can help explain, anticipate, and mitigate some sociotechnical harms

Inference: \(P_1 \land P_2 \to C_1\).

Jacobs and Wallach in Measurement and Fairness and present a (fairness) measurement framework that distinguishes debates about operationalization (assumptions) from debates about theoretical understanding of a construct (values) [1]. The argument formalizes the argument presented there. Jacobs and Wallach emphasize values also enter operationalization. The second premise asserts modeling can make operationalization assumptions explicit. The inference is that explicit modeling can identify mismatches between a theoretical construct and how it is measured; reducing some sociotechnical harms.

When is inference necessary?

Suppose you accept the premises. Their claim is usefulness, not necessity or guaranteed mitigation. There is still room for analysis. First, the assumption that a construct is actually unobservable is substantive. Second, the claim that a system must infer it is consequential.

Do researchers establish that their problem involves measuring an unobservable construct, or are they simply choosing to redescribe their problem in those terms? Establishing the former is consequential; it would imply some sociotechnical harms are unavoidable through construct-operationalization alone. Establishing the latter only indicates construct-operationalization can lead to mismatches that result in sociotechnical harms. That is a significantly different interpretation of the same argument.

Here is the key principle:

It is not necessary to directly challenge the observability of the construct. Instead, challenge the necessity of the inference.

Neither does specifying an operationalization establish that the construct can be inferred from observable indicators. Measurement modeling is a methodology for analyzing claims to measure constructs, not a necessary intermediary between theoretical understanding and systematic decision-making. Here is a useful distinguishing diagnostic that can be applied to a theoretical understanding of a construct:

  1. Observable: Is there an argument that the construct is unobservable?
  2. Descriptive: Is inferring it necessary to describe or justify the task?
  3. Warranted: Do the observable indicators warrant the inference?
  4. Proximate: Is the relevant harm explained by a construct-operationalization mismatch?

Diagnostic examples

The reliability of a construct-operationalization is distinct from the validity of its intended interpretation and use [1, §3]. The main purpose of the diagnostic is orthogonal: it is a probe regarding whether inference about an unobservable construct is necessary for the task, warranted, or useful for explaining a harm. Three examples from Jacobs and Wallach illustrate this distinction.

First, an example of a construct that is observable. Jacobs and Wallach use height as their first example of a construct writing that it is "not an unobservable theoretical construct" [1, §2.1]. This is intentional, they deliberately model it as latent for exposition. At this point, the diagnostic ends after the first question. Things like measurement error, shoes, and context-dependence can remain potentially descriptive, warranted, and proximate without establishing that latent inference is necessary.

Second, an example of an unobservable construct that the task need not infer. Socioeconomic status (SES) concerns an unobservable construct; "it cannot be measured directly and must instead be inferred from measurements of observable properties thought to be related to it" [1, §2.2]. However, the harm discussed concerns using monthly income assessments affected by payment timing [1, §2.2]. Such errors can be identified and corrected without estimating SES, as their income measurement-error model illustrates. The example demonstrates a consequential operationalization mismatch, but not the necessity of inferring the (SES) construct itself.

Third, an example where the evaluation system explicitly claims to measure a latent construct. Teacher effectiveness is unobservable, but evaluation claims to measure it [1, §2.3, §3.1]. One teacher's evaluation changes from 6 to 96 across two years. This indicates an unreliable measurement assuming underlying effectiveness remains comparably stable. However, the fact score unstable can be identified without discerning why: teacher effectiveness has changed, evaluations were noisy, or scores were biased. The validity of the score within an interpretation would still require considering what it was intended to measure.

These examples show measurement modeling can identify consequential assumptions, but do not establish that latent-construct inference is necessary to explain the harms or identify remedies. This raises a question about its distinctive explanatory contribution; should we work within a measurement framework by default or finding alternatives whenever possible?

Conclusions

Jacobs and Wallach rely on the claim that unobservable constructs require indirect measurement and that operationalization mismatches explain some fairness harms. However, this establishes the necessity of indirect inference only if measuring the construct is itself the goal. Their examples illustrate harmful assumptions about observable variables without establishing whether latent inference was necessary or whether the harm specifically resulted from a construct–operationalization mismatch.

This suggests construct–operationalization is particularly useful when a sociotechnical system is legitimized through claims to measure latent constructs. Teacher effectiveness, sycophancy, and toxicity all benefit from a shared vocabulary for describing operationalization failures. Such failures can cause real harms, but redescribing them as measurement problems does not establish that latent inference is necessary.

More broadly, shared abstractions involve a pragmatic trade-off between interpretability and grounding in the observable variables of a system. Stakeholders may have competing interests in describing a system through one construct rather than another, shaping what gets measured and ultimately optimized. Under such pressure, does evaluation evidence remain valid?
