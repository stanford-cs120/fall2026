When Do Audits Support Accountability?

Class meeting: 2026-11-17

Module 5: When Do Audits Support Accountability?

Audits examine AI systems and the institutions working on them. There is a growing prevalence of third-party auditing and assurance institutions [3]. This trend has been influenced by demand for independent assessment, external accountability, and support for enforcement of existing laws in a given jurisdiction [3–5]. Examples include the European Commission's European Centre for Algorithmic Transparency, which tests algorithmic systems and supports enforcement of the Digital Services Act [4], and AI Forensics, a European nonprofit that conducts independent algorithmic audits [5].

In the previous note we discussed construct operationalization as a framework for dealing with unobservable or hard-to-measure constructs. Here we ask how evaluation evidence enters institutional decisions. Who must respond to an audit finding, who has the authority to act, and what makes the institution accountable when a safeguard fails?

Raji et al. argue there is an accountability gap in development and deployment of AI systems [1]. While there may be a growing number of external auditing institutions, internal auditing remains challenging. Many harms from AI systems are only recognized after the relevant systems have been deployed. The core argument is that if the responsible development of AI is a core purpose of an organization creating AI, then a governance system through which the whole organization is held accountable should be established.

This note gives one perspective on what AI governance and accountability are about, and then uses Raji et al. to examine when audits support accountable governance within an institution. The main distinctions can be drawn concisely: governance directs and controls an institution; accountability concerns who must answer for decisions and their consequences; auditing examines compliance with respect to a set of standards. The central questions concern who must explain and justify their conduct, to whom, and what judgement or consequences can follow [2].

Governance, accountability, and auditing

ISO 37000:2021 defines governance of organizations as a "human-based system by which an organization is directed, overseen and held accountable for achieving its defined purpose" [6, §3.1.1]. The stipulation of a human-based system is important for AI governance: governance concerns the institutions and people responsible for directing and overseeing AI systems, not merely the technical mechanisms used to control their behavior. AI may support these processes, but ultimate organizational accountability lies with a governing body composed of people [6, §3.3.4].

This introduces a distinction between having responsibility and being held accountable. Responsibility refers to the state of obligation to act or make decisions, while accountability refers to the state of answerability for how those responsibilities are fulfilled [6, §§3.2.2–3.2.3]. Raji et al. use accountability broadly to mean "the state of being responsible or answerable for a system, its behavior and its potential impacts" [1, §2]. Bovens offers a narrower, relational perspective: an actor must explain and justify their conduct to a forum that can question, judge, and potentially impose consequences [2]. Assigning responsibility, then, does not by itself establish an effective accountability relationship.

An audit, whether internal or external, provides evidence that may be used to determine whether responsibilities have been fulfilled. For example, an audit may evaluate whether an organization is following its specified ethical standards. Those findings may reveal failures, but they do not establish accountability. Closing the gap between responsibility and accountability requires someone who is obligated to respond, a forum able to evaluate that response, and the possibility of consequences.

Internal auditing

Raji et al. introduce SMACTR, an internal audit framework consisting of five stages: Scoping, Mapping, Artifact Collection, Testing, and Reflection [1, §4]. This framework organizes several methods previously introduced in the course.

During scoping, the organization defines audit objectives alongside its ethical principles and potential harms of interest. This roughly corresponds to specifying system boundaries and assurance claims: defining the system, harms, and scope of the audit. In the mapping stage, a hazard-analysis method (e.g. FMEA) is used in conjunction with assessment against the organization's ethical principles. Following this, datasheets and model cards support artifact collection.

During testing, robustness evaluations are conducted. These may include group-disparity analysis and adversarial testing. Importantly, the documentation helps establish what those tests were intended to cover and what remains out of scope. In the last stage, reflection, an assurance claim is synthesized. The institutional assurance is not only about the safety-relevant claim. It also will specify who must respond to the findings, what decisions the assurance changes, and who is accountable for residual risk.

When do audits support accountability?

The audit is the process of obtaining decision-relevant evidence. Raji et al. also identify post-audit activities that are crucial to determining whether that audit supports accountability. These include go/no-go decisions, design mitigations, and tracking implementation. The audit helps close the accountability gap by making it explicit through evidence; it does not create accountability out of thin air.

Audits may struggle to support accountability when there are no mechanisms for enforcing decisions or imposing consequences on the relevant stakeholders. For example, suppose the audit results are sent to the upper-level members of an organization. The results expose internal problems within the organization. Groups may disagree about whether these problems are hazardous or warrant a safety constraint. One possible failure mode is that the audit identifies a problem and then nothing changes due to internal disagreement about how to translate the result into a governing decision. By default, the model or artifact gets released. Later, when there is an incident, the audit creates exposure for the organization by showing that it was aware of potential harms and did nothing.

Conclusions

In short, an audit is only as useful as the governance structure it is embedded in. Moreover, an organization's governance system is inherently human-based. An organization's AI ethical principles may be embedded in an auditing process, but this still does not determine who chooses these standards or has the power to revise them. This remains a social-choice problem.

In this way, the role of the audit might be better seen as a way to make the accountability gaps within an organization explicit. Audits support accountability by making values, principles, and decision criteria explicit and common knowledge within an organization. The slow and potentially politically costly process of administering an audit may also be something of a counterbalance to the comparatively rapid pace of AI development.
